# Suppressing tropical peatland and vegetation fires: science, practice, prioritisation

Research reference for the When Haze Stop "which hotspots to suppress first" feature. Compiled
2026-10-10 from peer-reviewed papers, agency doctrine and official statements, read through web
search and fetched documents.

## 1. Peat fire behaviour

### 1.1 Smouldering versus flaming

- [E] Peat fires are mostly **smouldering**: slow, low-temperature, flameless burning of a porous
  fuel. Peak temperature is about 500-700 C, against 1000-1500 C for flaming. The heat of
  combustion is about 5 kJ/g, and the fire creeps at about 1 mm/min. Sources: IPCC 2013 Wetlands
  Supplement, ch. 2, note to Table 2.7
  (https://www.ipcc-nggip.iges.or.jp/public/wetlands/pdf/Wetlands_separate_files/WS_Chp2_Drained_Inland_Organic_Soils.pdf);
  G. Rein, "Smouldering" lecture slides, 2023
  (https://ssfss2023.umd.edu/files/2023/06/Rein-Smouldering.pdf).
- [E] Rein calls smouldering fires "the easiest to ignite and the most difficult to suppress":
  - They ignite from a heat flux of 8 kW/m2, against 15 kW/m2 for flaming, and can self-heat at
    about 30 C.
  - They need more than 50% more water per kg of fuel to put out.
  - They keep burning down to about 10% oxygen, against 16% for flaming.
  - Smothering one takes "months vs. minutes".

  Source: Rein 2023, above.
- [E] A ground fire usually starts at the surface, then burns down through the pores of the peat,
  buried logs and roots, and spreads "vertically and horizontally, like a chimney of smoke". Wind
  barely affects it, so it spreads "unpredictably and slowly". White smoke is often the only
  visible sign, and toppled trees with green crowns give it away. Source: Manual for the Control of
  Fire in Peatlands and Peatland Forest, Adinugroho, Suryadiputra, Saharjo and Siboro, Wetlands
  International Indonesia Programme, 2005, sec. 2.3
  (https://luk.tsipil.ugm.ac.id/rawa/Buku-Panduan-Pengendalian-Kebakaran-Hutan-English-1.pdf).
- [E] Large burns also involve **flaming fires of surface vegetation on top of burning peat**.
  These are the most radiant parts of an episode and the heaviest smoke emitters. Source:
  Top-Down Estimation of Particulate Matter Emissions from Extreme Tropical Peatland Fires Using
  Geostationary Satellite FRP, Fisher et al., Sensors 20(24) 7075, 2020
  (https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/20/24/7075).

### 1.2 Spread rates

- [E] Lab, horizontal spread in milled peat: median 1-5 cm/h, slower as moisture and bulk density
  rise. That is about 0.25-1.2 m/day. Source: Prat-Guitart et al., Int. J. Wildland Fire,
  WF15103 (https://publish.csiro.au/wf/Fulltext/WF15103). [U] This is a lab result.
- [E] Lab, downward spread: about 1 cm/h in 30 cm moss-peat columns, slowing with depth. The rate
  is equivalent to a carbon flux of about 200 t C per day per hectare. Source: Downward spread of
  smouldering peat fire: the role of moisture, density and oxygen supply, Huang and Rein, Int. J.
  Wildland Fire 26(11) 907-918, 2017 (https://publish.csiro.au/WF/WF16198). [U] This is moss peat
  in the lab, not tropical field conditions.
- [E] Creep of about 1 mm/min, which is about 1.4 m/day (Rein 2023, above). A secondary source
  gives "about 1 m/day" for field creep (Wildfire Today, https://wildfiretoday.com/?p=9289).
- [E] Upward spread: smouldering takes about 2 weeks to climb through a 1 m layer, and "gives
  little hints of its presence until it is close to the surface". Source: Rein 2023, citing
  Palmer, Combustion and Flame, 1957.
- [I] Planning range: an established peat fire edge moves about **0.1-1.5 m/day**. A one-day delay
  barely changes its area, but each day it burns it emits heavily per hectare (sec. 2). Peat fires
  grow by persistence and by depth, not by racing across the landscape. The fast-moving danger is
  the flaming surface fire running over dry peat, which plants new smouldering ignitions.

### 1.3 Burn depth

- [E] Peat lost to the 1997 Central Kalimantan fires averaged more than half a metre across 43
  sites. Total release was 0.19-0.23 Gt C from peat and a further 0.05 Gt C from vegetation;
  extrapolated to all of Indonesia, 0.81-2.57 Gt C. Source: The amount of carbon released from peat
  and forest fires in Indonesia during 1997, Page et al., Nature 420, 61-65, 2002
  (https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v420y2002i6911d10.1038_nature01131.html).
- [E] Siegert and colleagues, 2002, report an average of 0.40 m lost over the same region.
- [E] LiDAR after the 2006 fires in Central Kalimantan gives a burn scar depth of 0.30 +/- 0.15 m.
  Source: Ballhorn and Siegert, EGU 2009 abstract
  (https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2009/EGU2009-7894.pdf).
- [E] UAV photogrammetry over a 2015 burn in Jambi gives a mean depth of about 0.23 m. The deepest
  burns, around tree roots, reach 1.3 m. Emissions were 134 +/- 29 t C/ha. Source: Tropical
  Peatland Burn Depth and Combustion Heterogeneity, Simpson et al., Remote Sensing 8(12) 1000, 2016
  (https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/8/12/1000). [U] The mean comes from a secondary slide deck.
- [E] Observed depth of burn ranges from 0.05 to 3 m, with a tropical average of 0.45 m. Burning
  into the ground consumes **50-100 times more fuel per unit area than flaming fires**. Source:
  Rein 2023.
- [E] Peat consumed falls with each repeat fire. Dry peat consumed is 206 t/ha in a first fire,
  115 in a second, 69 in a third and 23 after that, which is 114, 64, 38 and 13 t C/ha. Burn depth
  depends on fire frequency and on distance to drainage canals. Source: Variable carbon losses from
  recurrent fires in drained tropical peatlands, Konecny et al., Global Change Biology, 2016
  (https://figshare.le.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Variable_carbon_losses_from_recurrent_fires_in_drained_tropical_peatlands/10158473).
- [E] IPCC default fuel consumption for wildfire on drained tropical peat: **353 t dry matter/ha
  (95% CI 170-536)**. For prescribed agricultural burning on peat: 155 (82-228). The supplement
  says it found no literature for undrained tropical peat. Defaults are bulk density 0.1 g/cm3,
  with 0.09 used for tropical peat, and 50% carbon. Source: IPCC 2013 Wetlands Supplement,
  Table 2.6.
- [E] Two 2015 Kalimantan fires lost 102 m3/ha of peat in August and 754 m3/ha in September,
  which is 27.2 and 200.7 t/ha of gas emissions. Depending on the method, estimates swing from
  +798% to -26%. Source: Graham et al., cited via Fire 4(4) 64
  (https://www.doi.org/10.3390/fire4040064). The average used for a single event is about
  824 t CO2-eq/ha (Rodriguez Vasquez et al. 2021, cited by Trase,
  https://trase.earth/open-data/datasets/spatial-metrics-indonesia-palm-oil-emission-burned-peat-co2).

### 1.4 Water table, drainage and canals

- [E] Wosten et al. 2008 recommend keeping groundwater between 40 cm below and 100 cm above the
  peat surface. Below the -40 cm threshold, "fire susceptibility increases dramatically". Indonesian
  Government Regulation 71/2014 adopts 40 cm as the legal limit. Other studies put the onset of
  fire at about 50 cm (Atlantis Press GDIC-20) and the critical depth at about 74 cm (Febrianti
  2018). The depth of combustible peat rises linearly as groundwater falls. Sources:
  https://peatlands.org/document/peat-water-inter-relationships-in-a-tropical-peatland-ecosystem-in-south-east-asia/
  and https://atlantis-press.com/proceedings/gdic-20/125960283. [U] The threshold differs between
  sites.
- [E] Indonesia's SiPALAGA water-level network flags peat with groundwater below 40 cm as
  vulnerable. BMKG seeded clouds in West Kalimantan before the 2024 peak season to keep peat wet.
  Source: Controlling Fires in Peatlands and Peatland Forests in Southeast Asia, Silviana, Peteru
  and Dermawan, CIFOR-ICRAF, 2025
  (https://www.cifor-icraf.org/publications/pdf/manuals/Manual-Controlling-Fires-Peatlands.pdf).
- [E] Drainage makes peat easier to ignite and fires harder to detect and put out. Burn depth
  rises closer to canals (Konecny 2016).

### 1.5 Persistence, holdover and re-ignition

- [E] "Once ignited, smouldering peat fires burn for very long periods of time (e.g. months,
  years) despite extensive rains, weather changes or fire-fighting attempts." Source: The long slow
  burn of smouldering peat mega fires, Rein, IAWF, 2014
  (https://www.iawfonline.org/article/the-long-slow-burn-of-smouldering-peat-mega-fires/).
- [E] Often a peat fire is only fully out after heavy rain raises the groundwater.
  - The 2005 Wetlands manual says peat fires "can only be completely extinguished if the whole
    layer of burning peat is inundated", whether by pumps and needle sticks or by heavy rain.
  - Evans Road fire, North Carolina, 2008, lightning-started: about 16,500 ha burned, up to 1 m
    deep. Putting it out took about 7 months and more than 2 billion US gallons (about 7.6 billion
    litres) pumped, plus rain, at about USD 20 million.

    Sources: US Fish and Wildlife Service via Ducks Unlimited
    (https://ducks.org/conservation/conservation-projects/pocosin-lakes-nwr-2009); Rein 2023.
  - Las Tablas de Daimiel, Spain: burned from August 2009 until the heaviest winter rains in 50
    years stopped it in February 2010 (Rein 2023).
- [E] Holdover ("zombie") fires in boreal peat smoulder underground through winter and flare again
  in spring. Across Alaska and the Northwest Territories, 2002-2018, they made up 0.8% of burned
  area overall but **38% in an extreme year**. Source: Scholten et al., Nature, 2021, via CBC
  (https://www.cbc.ca/lite/story/1.7022806) and UAF
  (https://uaf-iarc.org/2021/05/19/scientific-evidence-found-of-overwintering-zombie-fires/).
  [I] The tropical equivalent is a dry-season carry-over: a fire persists under the surface between
  rains and between satellite detections, then resurfaces.
- [E] Re-ignition after partial suppression is measured:
  - Short water applications fail, and heat stored in the core of the peat re-ignites it (Fire
    Safety Journal 2017, Indonesian peat).
  - In the lab, 14 of 49 experiments kept smouldering. Temperature at depth climbed back to about
    220 C after falling to about 66 C. Re-ignition is possible whenever suppression stops above
    50 C, and another study found it at about 60 C.
  - **Infrared imaging could not detect the persistent smouldering at depth.**

  Source: Laboratory study on the suppression of smouldering peat wildfires: effects of flow rate
  and wetting agent, Santoso, Cui, Amin, Christensen, Nugroho and Rein, Int. J. Wildland Fire,
  2021 (https://connectsci.au/wf/article-lookup/doi/10.1071/WF20117).
- [E] An underground smouldering front can climb and resurface to ignite flaming litter. This is
  less likely when the peat is wetter or the litter is denser. Source: Fire Safety Journal, UI
  scholar listing
  (https://scholar.ui.ac.id/en/publications/experimental-study-of-the-effect-of-water-spray-on-the-spread-of-).
- [E] Field reports agree:
  - The South Sumatra task force ran 6,826 water-bombing operations in two months, and its field
    commander still said "the fire re-ignites" (The Jakarta Post, 2015,
    https://www.thejakartapost.com/news/2015/09/30/aerial-firefighting-strengthens-efforts-s-sumatra).
  - Firefighters describe fires coming back the morning after they seemed to be out (Oman Observer,
    https://www.omanobserver.om/ampArticle/24606).
  - In Aceh, July 2026, embers and subsurface fire were still present after 9 days on a fire of
    about 10 ha (RRI, https://rri.co.id/jakarta/nasional/2599388/).
- [E] Recurrence is high. About 1.3 Mha of the 2023 burned area, roughly 60%, had already burned
  between 2015 and 2022 (Greenpeace SEA, 2024,
  https://www.greenpeace.org/southeastasia/publication/66509/report-indonesias-chronic-forest-fires-2023/).
  About 12% of peatland in Sumatra and Kalimantan has burned more than once (Volkova et al. 2021).
  [U] The Greenpeace figure is advocacy analysis.

### 2.2 Totals and shares

- [E] 2015: fuel consumed was 358 Tg (+/-30%), and PM2.5 was **9.1 +/- 3.5 Tg, 95% of it from
  burning peatland**. About two-thirds came from Kalimantan and one-third from Sumatra. The peat
  diurnal cycle is about twice as wide as that of nearby non-peat fires and peaks much later in
  the day. Source: Wooster et al. 2018.
- [E] Peat burning was 68% of fire PM emissions in 2015 (9.4 Tg) and 45% over 2004-2015 (32 Tg
  total). Fires in Sumatra and Kalimantan caused 94% of fire PM in Equatorial Asia. Source: Air
  quality and health impacts of vegetation and peat fires in Equatorial Asia during 2004-2015,
  Kiely et al., Environ. Res. Lett., 2020
  (https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/ab9a6c).
- [E] Peatland covers less than 20% of Indonesia's land but produces about two-thirds of its fire
  emissions. Source: Fires, smoke exposure, and public health: an integrative framework, Marlier
  et al., GeoHealth, 2019 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7007093).

### 2.3 Per hectare: peat versus surface vegetation

[I] This is my arithmetic. It assumes fuel loads for surface vegetation that I did not source.

- **First fire on drained peat:** 353 t/ha (IPCC) x 24 g/kg is about **8.5 t PM2.5/ha**. Using
  Konecny's 206 t/ha instead gives about 4.9 t/ha.
- **Fourth or later fire on the same peat:** 23 t/ha x 24 g/kg is about 0.55 t/ha.
- **Surface vegetation on degraded peat or mineral soil:** if 5-30 t/ha of fern, shrub or grass
  burns at about 6-10 g/kg, that gives about 0.03-0.3 t PM2.5/ha.

So a hectare of first-time deep peat burn yields roughly **15-100+ times the PM2.5 of a hectare of
surface vegetation fire**. This agrees with Rein's 50-100x fuel consumption per area and the IPCC's
3-6x PM per kg. The direction is certain. The ratio is [U].

## 3. Which source regions matter for Kuala Lumpur

This is context for weighting fires. Transport, not just emissions, decides the haze in KL.

- [E] For Greater KL in September 2019, NAME dispersion modelling with GFAS emissions attributes
  **97% of fire PM2.5 to Riau and Jambi**:
  - Riau dominated at Klang (56%), Kuala Selangor (51%) and Bangi (56%).
  - Jambi dominated at Petaling Jaya (51%), Cheras (55%) and Putrajaya (44%).
  - South Sumatra contributed only 1-3%, even though Jambi, Riau and South Sumatra together emitted
    94% of September's emissions.

  Source: EGU24-8954 abstract
  (https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU24/EGU24-8954.html). [U] This is a conference
  abstract.
- [E] In 2006, Sumatran fires were 37% of regional emissions but 63% of population-weighted smoke
  exposure. "Protecting peatswamp forests in Southeast Sumatra emerges as a high priority."
  Source: Sensitivity of population smoke exposure to fire locations in Equatorial Asia, Kim,
  Jacob, Mickley, Koplitz et al., Atmospheric Environment 102, 11-17, 2015
  (https://www.ccacoalition.org/resources/sensitivity-population-smoke-exposure-fire-locations-equatorial-asia).
- [E] For the region's population centres, Sumatran emissions contribute more smoke than
  Kalimantan's, even where Kalimantan emits more than twice as much (Koplitz-coauthored work). The
  2015 haze caused about 100,300 excess deaths, much of the increase from fires in South Sumatra.
  Source: Koplitz et al., ERL 11, 094023, 2016
  (https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/public-health-impacts-of-the-severe-haze-in-equatorial-asia-in-se/).
- [E] For Singapore, 2004-2009: southern Sumatra contributed 42-62% of fire PM2.5, central Sumatra
  21-35%, and Kalimantan 14-15%. Source: Contribution of vegetation and peat fires to particulate
  air pollution in Southeast Asia, Reddington et al., ERL 9, 2014
  (https://doaj.org/article/17a12446a5e147b3a70b8c8811e832ff).
- [I] Under southwesterly flow (June to September, and into early October), fires in Riau and Jambi
  dominate the haze in KL. Kalimantan fires matter much less unless the wind changes. The ranking
  must use the forecast wind or a transport sensitivity, not emissions alone. Kim et al. used an
  adjoint; a cheap version is a back-trajectory footprint.

## 4. Suppression methods and how well they work on peat

### 4.1 How much water peat needs

- [E] Lab results for water per kg of peat:

  | Study | Water needed |
  |---|---|
  | Water injection, Papuan peat (UI study, 2022) | 4.22 +/- 0.26 L/kg |
  | Water injection, Jambi peat (UI study, 2022) | 3.81 +/- 0.03 L/kg |
  | Spray (Fire Safety Journal 2017) | about 6 L/kg |
  | Spray (Santoso et al. 2021) | **5.7 +/- 2.1 L/kg**, the same at every flow rate tested |
  | Earlier study cited by Santoso et al. | 6.4 L/kg |

  - Injection took about half as long as spraying and used less water.
  - Santoso et al. found the water needed stayed constant across flow rates. They read it as a
    "critical flooding point" of about 573% moisture: the water acts by cooling and flooding, not
    chemically.
  - A 1% plant-based wetting agent cut suppression time by 39%, and a 5% solution by 26%. The
    agent lowers surface tension from 72 to about 30 mN/m, which reduces channelling and runoff.

  Sources: Santoso et al. 2021; UI injection study
  (https://scholar.ui.ac.id/en/publications/experimental-study-on-peat-fire-suppression-through-water-injecti/).
- [E] Santoso et al. extrapolate to the field: on the order of **1 million litres per hectare**,
  about 580,000 L with a wetting agent, for their lab burn depth of about 8 cm. For a typical burn
  depth of about 39 cm, they say to multiply by about 5. GAMBUT field fires in Sumatra were sprayed
  at 3,000-4,900 L/h for 17-42 minutes.
- [E] An Indonesian expert told the President in September 2026 that one hectare needs
  "approximately 200,000 to one million litres", and asked to be corrected on the arithmetic.
  Source: Setkab
  (https://setkab.go.id/en/dialogue-between-president-of-the-republic-of-indonesia-and-academics-on-peatland-management-in-relation-to-forest-and-land-fire-potential-at-merdeka-palace-jakarta-monday-september-28-2026/).
- [E] A 2026 Fire Safety Journal study measured about 90 kg/m2 of water for air-dried peat, which
  is 900,000 L/ha. Source:
  https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/118323/1/1-s2.0-S0379711226001402-main.pdf.
- [I] Cross-checks:
  - 353 t/ha is 35 kg/m2, and at 4-6 L/kg that is about 1.4-2.1 million L/ha to flood the whole
    burned volume.
  - Evans Road actually used about 460,000 L/ha (7.6 billion L over 16,500 ha), helped by rain.
  - Working figure: **0.3-2 million litres per hectare of deep-burning peat**, about 30-200 mm of
    water over the area, that actually reaches the burning layer.

### 4.2 Aerial water bombing

- [E] Aircraft:
  - CL-415 scooper: 6,137 L per load, scooped in about 12 s over 400-1,300 m of water at least
    1.8 m (6 ft) deep. Sources: NIFC scooper plan
    (https://gacc.nifc.gov/nrcc/dispatch/aviation/ApprovedScooperOpsPlan.pdf); Canadian
    Encyclopedia (https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/canadair-cl-215).
  - Be-200: about 12,000-13,250 L per load and about 7-8 flights a day reported in South Sumatra,
    2015, flying only by day. Indonesia's own planes then carried about 5,000 L. Sources: The
    Jakarta Post
    (https://www.thejakartapost.com/news/2015/10/20/indonesia-brings-russian-aircraft-tackle-haze);
    Tempo (https://en.tempo.co/read/711704/two-russian-planes-land-in-palembang).
  - Helicopters: Malaysia's Bomba Mi-17 carries a 1,590 L Bambi bucket. Singapore's RSAF Chinook
    carried a 5,000 L bucket and discharged more than 400,000 L over two weeks in Sumatra in
    October 2015, reporting "more than 50 hotspots extinguished". Sources: MINDEF
    (https://www.mindef.gov.sg/news-and-events/latest-releases/2015oct24-news-releases-01712/);
    IGES (https://www.iges.or.jp/sites/default/files/2025-02/7_Samejima_IGES_202502a.pdf). [U]
    "Hotspots extinguished" was not verified as no re-ignition.
- [E] Turnaround: a CL-415 makes **6-10 drops per hour (37-61 kL/h)**. That falls to about 6 per
  hour with the water 10 nm away and about 9 per hour at 6 nm. A fuel cycle is about 4 hours.
- [E] Cost: helicopter rental in Indonesia is IDR 80-200 million per hour, about USD 5,000-12,500.
  In 2019, **about 70% of the national fire-mitigation budget, IDR 3.6 trillion of 6.7 trillion,
  went on helicopter rental**. A fire expert cites USD 2,000-3,000+ per hour. Sources: IGES 2025
  above; phys.org 2019 (https://phys.org/news/2019-08-forest.pdf).
- [E] Scale: in 2019 BNPB had 42 of about 50 helicopters in Riau, and in South Sumatra, October
  2019, 7 helicopters dropped 66 million litres. In August 2026 BNPB had 52 aircraft, 38 for water
  bombing. Sources: Setkab (https://setkab.go.id/en/?p=169136);
  https://kabar24.bisnis.com/read/20191014/15/1158844/.
- [E] Why it fails on peat:
  - Malaysia's Fire and Rescue director-general, 2015: "It is difficult to completely extinguish
    fire on peat land by using airplanes as it only sprays water from above... we need to flood
    the area" (NST,
    https://nst.com.my/amp/news/2015/10/105684/water-bombing-ineffective-fire-and-rescue-dept).
  - Indonesia's coordinating minister Luhut, 2015: water bombing "only managed to mitigate but not
    stop the fires" (The Jakarta Post).
  - A 2023 review: aerial drops are fast and reach remote areas but are expensive, risky and "less
    effective in extinguishing fire in the peat soil" (BALANGA,
    https://e-journal.upr.ac.id/index.php/JBL/article/view/11733).
  - Drops catch on the canopy and evaporate, and water runs off or channels through the peat
    (Santoso 2021).
- [E] General evidence: aircraft can be very effective at **initial attack**, but their effect on
  fires that have grown past that stage "has yet to be confirmed". Source: CSIRO on Plucinski's
  work, 2020
  (https://www.csiro.au/en/news/all/articles/2020/november/dr-matt-plucinski-studies-where-the-wildfires-are).
- [I] Arithmetic: one CL-415 flying 8 hours a day near a water source delivers about 300-500 kL.
  That is roughly the water for 0.2-1.5 ha of deep-burning peat, and only if every litre soaked
  into the burning layer. Realistically it is much less.

  Aircraft earn their cost by:
  - knocking down **flaming surface fires** running over peat, where PM2.5 is 44-61 g/kg;
  - wetting the ground ahead of a spreading edge;
  - protecting ground crews and villages;
  - first strikes on new, small, remote ignitions.

  They do not put out established deep peat fires.

### 4.3 Ground crews: pumps, lances and "jet shooting"

- [E] Doctrine for ground fires: push a needle stick, a perforated lance, into the smoking ground
  "until the peat fuel takes on the appearance of porridge". Keep probing until the fire is out.
  Dig out remnants under stumps with mattocks and spray again until no smoke remains. Inspect
  about one hour later. **Backburning is not recommended on peat**, because "it is extremely
  difficult to predict the direction in which peatland fires will spread". Mop-up is "essential in
  order to prevent fire from recurring". Source: Wetlands International manual, 2005, ch. 6.
- [E] Equipment in current Manggala Agni operations: Mark-3 pumps, mini strikers, hoses, jet
  shooters, nozzles and reservoirs, drawing water from nearby ditches. Source: RRI, July 2026.
  - A Mark-3-class pump delivers about 150-300 L/min (40-80 US gpm at 250-100 psi) through 1.5-inch
    hose (product listing,
    https://www.flashwildfireservices.ca/products/mark-3%c2%ae-essential-portable-high-pressure-fire-pump).
  - [I] One pump running 8 hours moves about 70-145 kL into the ground, the same order as one
    aircraft-day, but injected where it is needed.
- [E] Field rates of peat extinguishment, all [U] and taken from reports:

  | Operation | Result |
  |---|---|
  | Riau, Aug 2026: 434 ha peat, 4 pumps, 16 days | **about 45 ha out** (about 2.8 ha/day for the whole operation); 2 ha on day 16 |
  | Riau joint team of more than 20 people | about 4 ha by one reported date |
  | Injection-pipe method (South African experts) | a 100 x 25 m patch (0.25 ha) fully out in 6 h |
  | Manggala Agni crew, Sulawesi (crew size not given) | 5 m2 in 2 hours of continuous work |
  | Wetlands 2005 case, Central Kalimantan | 15-20 people per day on a multi-agency effort |

  Sources: The Star, 18 Aug 2026
  (https://www.thestar.com.my/aseanplus/aseanplus-news/2026/08/18/riau-peatland-fire-still-burning-after-16-days-scorches-a-whopping-434-hectares);
  Antara/Kalsel (https://kalsel.antaranews.com/berita/30314/); VICE
  (https://vice.com/en/article/meet-the-people-who-put-out-indonesias-forest-fires).
- [E] Water access is the limiting constraint. In the Riau case, water 300-500 m away hindered the
  work. Field guidance is to map water sources in the dry season, before fires start, along with
  wells and *beje* ponds. Sources: The Star above; Wetlands manual sec. 5.2.
- [E] Malaysia's own practice on peat fires in Selangor and Johor uses tube wells. The Department
  of Mineral and Geoscience (JMG) runs 24 of them across 9 locations, including Kuala Langat. They
  pump groundwater into drains to flood the peat, and cost about RM 0.38 million per well
  (RM 2.3 million for 6). Malaysia has offered tube wells to Indonesia. Sources: Borneo Post 2019
  (https://www.theborneopost.com/2019/03/29/rm2-3-million-for-building-six-tube-wells-to-fight-peat-fires/);
  Malay Mail 2020; Eco-Business
  (https://www.eco-business.com/news/malaysia-to-introduce-tube-wells-to-indonesia-to-fight-haze-causing-peat-soil-fires/).
- [I] Planning productivity for one ground team (about 10-20 people, 1-4 pumps, water within a few
  hundred metres): **about 0.25-3 ha of deep-burning peat fully out per day**. Without a water
  source it is near zero. On surface-only fires it is much higher.

### 4.4 Surfactants and other agents

- [E] Wetting agents reduce suppression time by 26-39% (Santoso 2021). In the field, a 1% soap
  solution brought peat surface temperature down to about 66 C, against about 160 C with water
  alone (Fire Technology 2023, Palangka Raya, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10694-023-01381-z).
- [E] Fluorinated foams may leave peat water-repellent and hinder rewetting (Santoso 2021).
- [E] Rein lists the options for a fire that is large and found late, in this order:
  1. Flooding.
  2. **Compartmentation by firebreaks.**
  3. Wetting with additives.
  4. Lance injection.
  5. Foams.
  6. Inerting the fuel, for example with sand.
  7. Compaction.
  8. Sealing, which takes about 100 days.
  9. Smothering below 10% oxygen.

  His first principle is "detect early, then locate the hotspots, and deliver suppression".

### 4.5 Firebreaks and compartments

- [E] Peat firebreaks work as trenches dug down to the water table or to mineral soil, often using
  canals and *beje* ponds, with heavy equipment where fires burn deep (CIFOR-ICRAF 2025; Alberta
  practice reported by CBC, https://www.cbc.ca/1.5197356). [U] I did not find a sourced trench
  specification for Indonesia.

### 4.6 Canal blocking and rewetting (prevention, slow)

- [E] Where rewetting worked across about 4,000 km2, hotspots fell by **up to 22%** between 2015
  and 2019. Deep wells did better overall. Canal blocks did best when sited well and built in
  clusters. But **70% of canal-block units were damaged** by 2015-2024, and only blocks about a
  year old reliably kept peat wet. Source: Salmayenti et al., GRL, 2026, via The Star
  (https://www.thestar.com.my/aseanplus/aseanplus-news/2026/08/19/comment-indonesias-peatlands-show-restoration-can-work).
  [U] I saw only the press summary.
- [E] A counterfactual for West and Central Kalimantan, 2017-2022, finds that even if every block
  worked, rewetting would avoid at most **6.4% of burned area**. Concrete blocks built in 2017
  stopped fire in their rewetted zones in 2019, while wooden blocks raised fire by up to 24%.
  Sources: Castro et al., iScience, 2026; EGU23-10738
  (https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU23/EGU23-10738.html).
- [I] Rewetting is a lever for next season, not for this week's assistance force. It does matter
  for priority, though: fires on blocked, rewetted peat should burn shallower.

### 4.7 Weather modification (TMC, cloud seeding)

- [E] A 12-day BRIN operation in Riau in August 2022 estimated rainfall gains of 56.9% by a
  target-only method and 81.1% by a target-control method. Gains were larger in a La Nina year.
  Source: BRIN, Jurnal Sains & Teknologi Modifikasi Cuaca 24(1), 2023
  (https://ejournal.brin.go.id/JSTMC/article/view/17083).
- [E] Studies report fewer hotspots after seeding only qualitatively (Riau 2017, Jambi 2012). Local
  officials in 2023 said the rains were "still not enough to extinguish the fires completely".
  Seeding needs seedable clouds, which are scarce in El Nino droughts. Sources: Antara Mataram
  (https://mataram.antaranews.com/berita/279051/); CIFOR-ICRAF 2025.
- [I] TMC is a weather-dependent, area-wide multiplier that works best before the peak, to keep the
  water table up. It is not an asset to point at a single hotspot. It is the only method whose
  effect matches the hydrological scale of peat fires, because heavy rain is what actually puts
  them out (sec. 1.5).

### 4.8 Summary by method

| Method | On deep peat | On flaming surface fire over peat | Cost and logistics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed-wing scooper (CL-415, Be-200) | Low; drops do not reach the burning layer [E] | Good at initial attack [E] | High; needs a scoopable water body within about 10 nm, daylight only |
| Helicopter bucket (1.6-5 kL) | Low [E] | Moderate to good | USD 5-12.5k per hour; 70% of Indonesia's 2019 budget [E] |
| Ground pump + lance or injection | **The only reliable direct method** [E] | Good | Cheap per litre; limited by access and water distance |
| Flooding via canals or tube wells | High where feasible [E] | n/a | Needs infrastructure and water |
| Firebreak or trench to water table | Contains spread [E] | Contains | Heavy equipment or many crew-days |
| Wetting agent | Cuts time 26-39% [E] | Helps | Logistics of the concentrate |
| TMC | High if heavy rain results; weather-dependent [I] | High | Needs clouds |
| Canal blocking | Up to 22% fewer hotspots, needs maintenance [E] | n/a | Seasonal or annual horizon |

## 5. Prioritisation doctrine and the operations-research literature

### 5.1 Agency doctrine

- [E] **Initial attack.** Most fires are contained small, and the few that escape cause most of
  the impact and cost.
  - US Pacific Northwest, 2023: 97% initial-attack success, with 25 of 926 fires growing past
    100 acres (USDA FS, https://www.fs.usda.gov/media/30776).
  - Ontario: 79-98% success depending on the definition used (Int. J. Wildland Fire WF24104,
    https://publish.csiro.au/WF/fulltext/WF24104).
  - Alberta, more than 13,000 fires: adding 64 crew work-hours raised initial-attack success on
    medium-difficulty fires from 92.2% to 96.2%. Escaped fires were assessed at an average of
    2.7 ha and grew to about 408 ha (U Alberta, 2023,
    https://www.ualberta.ca/news/news-releases-and-statements/news-releases/2023/08-august/stacking-firefighters-on-the-front-line-of-a-wildfire-critical-in-first-hours-u-of-a-study.html).
  - Victoria: the small share of fires that escape initial attack cause the greatest community
    impact and most of the cost (Int. J. Wildland Fire WF23053).
- [E] **Ranking competing fires.** US multi-agency practice ranks firefighter and public safety
  first. Then come values at risk (communities, property, infrastructure, timber, watersheds,
  habitat) and the **probability of success**. Resources go "where success was probable". The
  Northwest MAC Incident Prioritization Worksheet scores each value group 1-5. Spending should be
  in proportion to the values at risk. Source: NW MAC IPW instructions, 2015
  (https://gacc.nifc.gov/nwcc/content/products/nwmac/2015%20Revision--Instructions%20for%20Completing%20the%20NW%20MAC%20IPW.pdf).
- [E] **Anchor and flank.** Start fireline from an anchor, a barrier to spread, then work along the
  flanks toward the head. **Containment is not control.** Containment percentage measures how much
  of the perimeter has line, and a contained fire may still be burning inside. A fire is
  controlled only when it is contained and crews have put out smouldering fuel 100-300 ft inside
  the perimeter. Sources: USFS statement via AP/WHYY
  (https://whyy.org/articles/wildfire-terms-definitions-contained-extinguished-mopping-up/); Alberta
  Wildfire via CBC. A South African standard requires guarding a controlled line for at least 48 h
  (https://d7.westerncape.gov.za/sites/www.westerncape.gov.za/files/annexure_h_2_mopping-up_standard_2017_18_9.pdf).
- [E] **Weather beats size.** For large US fires, the chance of containment rose with consecutive
  days of low growth and fell during high-spread intervals and in timber fuels. **Fire size was not
  a significant predictor.** Source: Modeling containment of large wildfires using generalized
  linear mixed-model analysis, Finney, Grenfell and McHugh, Forest Science 55(3), 2009
  (https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/33778).
- [E] **Peat-specific doctrine:**
  - Act as early as possible.
  - Map water sources in the dry season.
  - Use ground crews with pumps and needle sticks.
  - Mop up to complete extinction, then inspect.
  - Do not backburn.
  - Community fire brigades exist to hit fires before outside teams can arrive.

  Sources: Wetlands International 2005; CIFOR-ICRAF 2025.

  Fire danger tables in the same manuals tie a high Drought Code to "live embers smouldering" and
  to fires that are "extremely difficult to control".

### 5.2 Operations research

- [E] Initial-attack allocation as integer programming:
  - Donovan and Rideout, Forest Science 49, 2003: minimise cost plus net value change, subject to
    a budget.
  - Rideout, Wei and Kirsch, Int. J. Safety and Security Eng. 1(3), 2011: allocation to multiple
    simultaneous fires, with a proxy cost for fires that escape
    (https://www.witpress.com/elibrary/sse/1/3/560).
  - Probabilistically constrained stochastic integer programs (Int. J. Wildland Fire WF13204).
  - Martell 1982 deployment models, and Boychuk and Martell 1988 Markov cost-plus-loss models.
- [E] Spatial suppression placement: Belval, Wei and Bevers place suppression on a raster with
  fire spread paths and arrival times. Their deterministic MIP is in Can. J. For. Res. 45(4), 2015,
  and the stochastic version with uncertain weather in 46(2), 2016
  (https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/48943). Wei et al. built MIPs that group Potential
  Operational Delineations (PODs) to minimise expected loss for large fires.
- [E] Graph models: in Hartnell's Firefighter problem (1995), you protect k vertices per turn to
  limit spread on a graph. On trees, greedy gives a 1/2-approximation, LP rounding gives 1 - 1/e
  (Cai, Verbin and Yang), and there is a PTAS (arXiv 1601.00271). Anshelevich et al. cast it as
  monotone submodular maximisation. A Containment Protocol that looks two steps ahead is near
  optimal on grids. Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1601.00271.pdf.
- [E] Reviews: Plucinski 2019, Current Forestry Reports 5, 1-19 and 20-40, says suppression
  effectiveness is hard to measure. Productivity models built from non-wildfire data "grossly
  over-estimate resource efficiency" (CSIRO PyroPage 22, 2019). [U] Martell, "A review of recent
  forest and wildland fire management decision support systems research", Current Forestry Reports
  1, 2015, is cited from memory and not checked in this session.
- [E] No published optimisation model targets Indonesian peat fires with a haze objective; none
  turned up in searching. The SMOKE Policy Tool (Marlier et al. 2019, built with Indonesia's
  peatland restoration agency BRG) ranks *prevention and restoration* locations by the exposure
  they avoid. It is the closest precedent.
- [I] How our problem maps onto these:
  - Objective: maximise expected PM2.5-exposure-hours avoided in KL.
  - Decisions: assign K teams (and A aircraft) to fire clusters.
  - The marginal value of a team on a cluster has diminishing returns. A cluster is worth almost
    nothing until it is fully extinguished or its spreading edge is held, then a lot.
  - So the value is **not additive per hotspot**. A greedy rule over whole clusters is a sensible
    heuristic; a greedy rule over individual hotspots is not.
  - Value per resource-day = (expected avoided emissions x transport weight to KL) / (team-days
    needed to finish).

## 6. Why "kill the biggest flame" or "kill the middle of the cluster" fails

1. **Partial suppression buys almost nothing.**
   - [E] Peat re-ignites from stored heat when suppression stops above about 50-60 C (Santoso
     2021).
   - [E] Deep smouldering is invisible even to infrared (Santoso 2021).
   - [E] South Sumatra ran 6,826 water-bombing sorties and the fires "re-ignite".
   - [I] A hotspot that "goes dark" after a drop is not out. Count it as suppressed only after
     ground probing, or after it stays undetected for several nights while dry.
2. **Neighbours re-ignite treated ground.**
   - [E] Smouldering spreads underground at about 0.1-1.5 m/day and resurfaces to ignite litter.
     Flaming surface fires over peat spread fast and seed new smouldering.
   - [E] Doctrine requires anchoring and holding a perimeter. Interior islands and edges left
     alight re-burn treated ground.
   - [I] Putting out the middle of a cluster leaves the edges burning, and they burn back inward
     and outward. Treated peat stays at risk because its water table is still low.
3. **The biggest flame is not the biggest haze source.**
   - [E] FRP tracks flaming. Smouldering peat has low FRP but a high PM yield.
   - [E] Fisher 2020 found the smoke emission coefficient (g PM per MJ) for the most radiant peat
     fires about 3 times *lower* than the default (16.8 vs 52.4 g/MJ): per unit of radiated energy,
     they emit less smoke than assumed.
   - [E] Fires get harder to see as they intensify. "When Indonesian fires become the most intense,
     the number of fires recorded by VIIRS or MODIS can actually decrease," because of smoke
     (NASA, https://science.nasa.gov/?p=1283342).
   - [I] Ranking by FRP over-ranks short surface fires on mineral soil and under-ranks long-lived
     smouldering deep peat.
4. **Large established fires are weather-limited.**
   - [E] Containment of large fires depends on low-growth days, not on fire size (Finney 2009).
   - [E] Peat fires end with heavy rain (Evans Road, Las Tablas, the Wetlands manual).
   - [I] A large mature peat fire takes a very large team-time to finish. The same resources on
     several small new fires prevent several future large ones.
5. **Holdover and recurrence.**
   - [E] Holdover fires made up 38% of burned area in an extreme boreal year.
   - [E] About 60% of the 2023 Indonesian burned area had burned before.
   - [I] Fires that are not finished come back within the same season.
6. **Exceptions where attacking a large fire is right.** [I]
   - Its spreading flank is about to enter never-burned, deep, drained peat upwind of KL.
   - It threatens people.
   - A wind change is forecast to put it on the KL trajectory.

   In these cases the doctrine is *flank and edge first*: hold the side advancing into fresh fuel
   and leave the burned-out interior.

## 7. Detection: what a FIRMS hotspot means

- [E] **Pixel size:** about 1 km for MODIS and about 375 m for VIIRS. Pixels grow toward the edge
  of the swath. A detection is the **centre of a pixel** containing one or more thermal anomalies.
  It is not the fire's exact position or its burned area, and "hotspot points should not be used
  to estimate burned area". A line of detections usually marks a fire front. Source: NASA FIRMS
  FAQ (https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/tools/firms/faq).
- [E] **Overpass times, local mean time:**

  | Satellite | Day | Night |
  |---|---|---|
  | Terra | about 10:30 | about 22:30 |
  | Aqua | about 13:30 | about 01:30 |
  | Suomi NPP | about 13:30 | about 01:30 |
  | NOAA-20 / NOAA-21 | about 12:40 | about 00:40 |

  **New Suomi NPP observations stop on 2 November 2026**, so pipelines should use NOAA-20 and
  NOAA-21. Near-real-time data arrive within about 3 hours. Source: FIRMS FAQ.
- [E] **Smallest fire detected:** MODIS routinely detects flaming and smouldering fires of about
  1,000 m2, and flaming fires of about 100 m2 in very good conditions. The VIIRS 375 m algorithm is
  tuned for small fires (FIRMS FAQ; NASA LANCE VJ114IMGTDL_NRT,
  https://modaps.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/services/about/products/viirs-land-c2-nrt/vj114imgtdl_nrt.html).
- [E] **Confidence:**
  - MODIS gives 0-100%, grouped into low, nominal and high.
  - VIIRS gives low, nominal or high.
    - Low: daytime sun glint, or a temperature anomaly under 15 K in channel I4.
    - Nominal: no glint and an anomaly over 15 K.
    - High: saturated pixels, day or night.
  - Sources: GEE community catalog (https://gee-community-catalog.org/projects/firms_vector/);
    FIRMS FAQ.
- [E] **FRP:** the radiative power of all fire in the pixel, in MW. The 375 m product's FRP comes
  from a hybrid of 375 m and 750 m data; the NOAA-20 product notes it is detection-only at 375 m
  because of saturation. [U] The documentation disagrees on this point.
- [E] **From FRP to emissions:** fire radiative energy (FRE) is FRP integrated over time, and is
  proportional to the fuel burned. The lab and field coefficient is 0.368-0.453 kg/MJ for
  vegetation, from Wooster et al. 2005, JGR 110, D24311
  (https://gaftp.epa.gov/Air/aqmg/baker/info/Journal%20of%20Geophysical%20Research%20%20Atmospheres%20-%202005%20-%20Wooster.pdf).
  Satellite-derived values vary: about 0.27-0.37 kg/MJ for MODIS and GOES over the conterminous US,
  and 1.37-4.5 kg/MJ implied by aerosol optical depth constraints. Source: Li et al. 2018 and an
  AGU 2015 abstract (https://agu.confex.com/agu/fm15/webprogram/Paper64639.html). Twice-daily polar
  snapshots cannot integrate FRE. Geostationary data (Himawari, every 10 minutes) are needed for
  that, and are what Wooster 2018 and Fisher 2020 used.
- [E] **Caveats for peat:**
  - Smouldering is cooler and smaller, so it is harder to detect. Operational products report one
    "active fire" class and do not separate smouldering from flaming (Sofan et al., Landsat-8
    studies).
  - VIIRS night data matched Landsat peat-combustion maps better than day data, but in one study
    VNP14IMG detected only 71% of fires against ground truth.
  - Peat fires peak later in the day and have a cycle about twice as wide as nearby non-peat fires
    (Wooster 2018), so a 13:30 snapshot under-samples them.
  - Clouds, smoke and canopy hide fires. Understory and underground fires are "very unlikely" to be
    detected (FIRMS FAQ).
  - The IPCC notes that the low temperatures of organic-soil fires "make them harder to detect by
    satellites".
- [I] What this means for the product:
  - Treat **repeated detections at the same spot across several days, especially at night**, on
    mapped peat as the signature of a deep, persistent, high-emission fire, whatever its FRP.
  - Treat **high FRP on peat** as a flaming surface fire, the highest PM per kg (44-61 g/kg), which
    is also seeding new ground fires. It is a strong initial-attack target for aircraft.
  - Treat **one low-FRP daytime detection off peat** as low haze value.
  - Never read "no detection" as "out".

## 8. Decision rules (all [I]: syntheses of sections 1-7 for agents to use)

1. **Score by haze delivered to KL, not by flame size.** Haze value = emission potential x
   transport weight x expected remaining burn duration.
   - Emission potential: on peat or not; peat depth or carbon; drained (canal distance) or
     rewetted; first burn or repeat burn; flaming surface fire over peat or not.
   - Transport weight: the forecast wind or a trajectory footprint to KL. Riau and Jambi dominate
     in southwesterly flow.
   - Remaining duration: weeks for deep peat in drought unless heavy rain is forecast.
2. **Prefer new, small fires on deep drained peat that teams can reach and water.** These have the
   highest marginal return:
   - fresh peat fires emit about 58 g/kg, falling about 9% a day;
   - first burns consume about 200-350 t/ha;
   - a small fire can be *finished*.

   Initial attack prevents a weeks-long burn.
3. **Rank by completion, not by contact.** Give resources only to fires the force can *finish*, or
   whose advancing edge it can *hold*, within the window before conditions change. A
   half-suppressed peat fire is worth roughly zero.
4. **Treat clusters as single incidents.** Merge detections within about 1-2 km, or in the same peat
   dome or hydrological unit, into one incident. Assign whole incidents, or their advancing
   perimeter. Never pick the hotspot in the middle of an unsuppressed cluster.
5. **On large, established peat fires, hold the edge, don't fight the interior.** Hold the flank
   advancing into unburned deep peat, or toward people or a trajectory change. Leave the burned-out
   interior and repeat-burned ground until rain comes.
6. **Match each asset to its job:**
   - Aircraft: flaming surface fires on peat, initial attack on new remote ignitions, perimeter
     wetting, protecting crews. Scoopers need water within about 10 nm, flying in daylight.
   - Ground pump and lance teams: actual extinction of deep peat.
   - TMC: area-wide and only when clouds allow.
7. **Water access is a hard constraint.** A fire more than a few hundred metres from water (canal,
   river, *beje*, well) with no other way to get water there is a poor target for ground teams.
   Plan on 0.3-2 million L per hectare of deep-burning peat, and 0.25-3 ha per team-day.
8. **Budget for mop-up and patrol.** Count a fire as out only after ground probing shows the peat
   below about 50 C, or after several dry nights with no detection. Keep patrolling: smouldering
   takes about 2 weeks to climb 1 m. Reserve capacity for re-ignitions; do not commit everything to
   new contacts.
9. **Read FRP correctly.** Use high FRP on peat to flag flaming surface fires. Use persistence over
   several nights on peat to flag deep fires. Never use summed FRP as the haze ranking for peat.
10. **Heavy rain in the forecast changes the calculus.** If heavy rain is expected over a fire
    within a few days, its expected remaining emissions fall sharply. Move resources to fires the
    rain will miss.

## 9. Uncertainty, gaps and what I could not verify

  controlled field study I could find. They come from lab extrapolation, news reports and one US
  case. Treat 0.25-3 ha/day and 0.3-2 ML/ha as planning ranges, accurate to a factor of a few.
- **The per-hectare PM2.5 ratio** of peat to surface fires (section 2.3) is my arithmetic. The
  surface fuel loads in it are assumptions.
- **No Indonesian operational standard** (KLHK/Manggala Agni) for lance depth, pump flow, or
  firebreak trench depth was retrieved.
- **I could not reach these full texts:** Usup et al. 2004 (Tropics 14(1), field spread rates in
  Central Kalimantan), the Kim et al. 2015 figures for Peninsular Malaysia, and the full Wooster
  2018 paper (peat FRE coefficient).
- **The KL source shares** (Riau and Jambi 97%) come from one conference abstract for one month,
  September 2019.
- **The canal-blocking figures** come from 2026 press and abstract summaries.
- **The web-search budget ran out** before checking: Malaysia's own 2015 CL-415 sorties in Sumatra;
  ASEAN assistance procedures under the Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution; and fireline
  intensity limits for aircraft. On those limits, CSIRO gives about 800-1000 kW/m for hand tools
  and about 2000 kW/m for direct attack by machines and aircraft (https://curious.science.org.au/node/836).
